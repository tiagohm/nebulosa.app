# Observing Planner Contract

Part of the [architecture](../ARCHITECTURE.md) of Nebulosa.

- The Observing Planner (`api/src/observingplanner.ts` with `observingplanner.astronomy.ts`, `observingplanner.minorbodies.ts`, `observingplanner.minorbodies.store.ts`, `observingplanner.schedule.ts`, and `observingplanner.store.ts`) finds and analyzes targets over a night of a site and keeps the night plans the operator writes. Catalog objects, major bodies, and coordinates are computed locally: the embedded sky catalog, the toolkit's analytical models (VSOP87E, ELPMPP02, and the Pluto model through `bodyModel` and `geocentricInterpolation` of `api/src/sky/ephemeris.ts`), and the Sun night of Astro Weather (`AstroWeatherService.night`, called in process). Only a minor body is asked of anything else, on demand when it is analyzed: the SPK kernels of the SPICE Kernel Library, and SBDB and Horizons through the shared JPL queue when the ephemeris policy allows (see Minor bodies below). It never asks a forecast or a device, never starts an operation or a session, and moves no mount, Sky clock, profile, or site. Plans are documents to read and edit, not runnable sequences.
- Targets (`ObservingTargetRef`) are a catalog object by id, a major body by Horizons code (the Moon, the planets, and Pluto; never the Sun), a fixed ICRS/J2000 place with a name, or a minor body (`{ kind: 'minorBody', spkId, designation }`, the shape of the Sky's `SkyObjectRef`) by its canonical SBDB SPK-ID, decimal text never compared as a number; two minor bodies are the same by SPK-ID alone, whatever designation named them. Satellites and constellations are not targets. A catalog id the catalog no longer knows, or an SPK-ID SBDB does not know, is `unresolved`, never replaced by another object or by stored coordinates; a moving body is never stored or analyzed as fixed coordinates.
- Minor bodies (`MinorBodyTrajectories`): the trajectory of a night comes from one source for the whole night, chosen by the ephemeris policy read once: the SPK kernels when they resolve the body and the Earth at every node (`spkOnly` allows nothing else); then under `automatic` a geometric Horizons vector table (`VECTOR`, centered on the Earth, ICRF equator, no corrections) through the JPL queue, and the two-body Kepler model of the SBDB elements (`keplerOrbit`, with the Sun and the Earth of VSOP87E) when Horizons fails, stating `fallbackReason` (`timeout` or `network`); under `offline` the Kepler model of elements already known (the SBDB cache in memory or those saved with a plan), with `fallbackReason: 'offline'`, never asking JPL. Nodes of two sources are never mixed, and a kernel that cannot be read is reported rather than replaced. The trajectory is materialized as geometric geocentric ICRS vectors (no light-time, like the planets) every 10 minutes over the night, or every minute when the second differences of the nodes say linear interpolation errs by more than 1″, interpolated linearly, with the observer's offset removed at each instant (`PlanningNight.topocentric`); evaluating it never reads a kernel, asks JPL, or queries the database. Its root-finder step is 10 minutes, shortened to whole minutes (at least one) so the body moves no more than 2.5° against the stars within a step. A body no source covers, or too fast to follow within those limits, is `ephemerisUnavailable` with one reason (`orbitalSolutionUnavailable`, `kernelCoverageUnavailable`, `ephemerisOffline`, `ephemerisSourceUnavailable`, `trajectoryResolutionExceeded`), answered `200` like `unresolved`, never `notObservable`. Its analysis adds `ephemeris` (`PlannerEphemerisProvenance`: the source, `approximate` for Kepler, the kernels and their generation, the epoch, solution date, and reading time of the elements, the node spacing, and the search step), `motion` (the highest topocentric rate against the stars, radians per minute), and the distance and estimated magnitude (H–G or M1–K1 from the SBDB photometry, absent without it) of each sample. A two-body orbit ignores the planets, so its error grows with the age of the elements; no accuracy is promised for it. Prepared trajectories (32) are kept in memory by body, night, policy, kernel generation, and the reading times of the elements, except a Kepler fallback after a failed Horizons request, which is asked again next time; a cancelled request stops preparing and answers nothing.
- Saved elements (`observing_minor_bodies`, `ObservingMinorBodyStore`): after every committed save, duplicate, or deletion of a plan, the elements SBDB answered (in memory) for each minor body a plan names but none is saved for are saved, and those no plan names are removed, so a saved plan can still be analyzed offline and after a restart. `POST /minor-bodies/:spkId/refresh` (`ObservingMinorBodyRefresh`) reads a body again from SBDB and saves it, only under the `automatic` policy (`503 offline` otherwise), `404 notFound` for a body SBDB does not know, and `503 serviceUnavailable` when SBDB or the queue refuses; nothing refreshes by itself, and saving or refreshing elements never changes a plan, its revision, or its events.
- A night is named by the civil date of its evening in the site's zone (`eveningDate`, YYYY-MM-DD): `zonedDate` of `api/src/sky/sun.ts` gives the bounds of that date, and the night is the Astro Weather night containing its last instant, from the Sun's upper transit of that date to the next one, checked against `sun.localDate`. Times are Unix milliseconds (UTC) throughout; a display zone only formats them.
- Geometry (`PlanningNight`): the directions given to `visibilityWindows` are ICRS vectors, never coordinates of date. A catalog object is its J2000 place moved by its proper motion to the middle of the night, coordinates are taken as they are, and the Sun, the Moon, and the bodies are geometric geocentric vectors evaluated at each whole UTC hour and interpolated, minus the observer's offset, so the Moon's altitude and separations are topocentric. Every instant of a night carries a fixed precession-nutation matrix and an apparent sidereal time advanced at the sidereal rate, both of the night's middle (`TimeProviders`), which keeps the error under 0.1″ and makes an analysis about a hundred times cheaper. Altitudes are geometric (airless) for the constraints, the airmass (Kasten–Young), the samples, and the score; no refraction, horizon mask, or terrain is applied, and the horizon is the geometric one.
- Constraints (`ObservingConstraints`, `OBSERVING_PRESETS`, served by `GET /presets`): a mode (`deepSky`, `visual`, `planetary`), a minimum altitude, an optional maximum airmass, a maximum solar altitude, an optional minimum lunar separation enforced only while the Moon is above the geometric horizon (`moonSeparationHorizon` of the toolkit), and a minimum continuous duration of at least 10 minutes, the search step, so a window the search could miss could never qualify. The presets are documented heuristics, not equipment limits.
- Analysis (`POST /analyze`, `TargetAnalysis`): the windows are the stretches of the night satisfying every constraint, found by the toolkit's root search and kept when they last the minimum duration. The status is `observable` (a window as long as the requested duration), `limitedDuration` (only shorter windows, reason `shorterThanRequested`), `notObservable`, or `unresolved`. When there is no window, the reasons come from adding one constraint at a time: `noAstronomicalDarkness` or `sunTooHigh`, `belowMinimumAltitude`, `airmassTooHigh`, `notDuringDarkness`, `moonTooClose`, `windowTooShort`. The culmination is the highest altitude over the night, labeled apart from any meridian transit. Samples every 10 minutes over the night's `display` draw the chart; crossings always come from the windows.
- Score (`ObservingScore`, model version 1): computed only inside windows, as the best `observationScore` of the toolkit (0–100) at the samples of a window and its middle; the factors of `observationScoreFactors` are returned by name. The altitude factor rises from the minimum altitude, the airmass factor falls to zero at the airmass limit (or airmass 3), and the duration factor compares the window with the requested duration. Twilight and the Moon (`moonInterference` with the illuminated fraction) count in the deep-sky and visual modes, not in planetary. It is a relative ranking heuristic, never a predicted signal-to-noise ratio or sky brightness, and it never makes an unobservable target eligible.
- Catalog and discovery: `GET /catalog` lists the catalog through `queryCatalog` (text against designations and proper names, classes galaxy, nebula, cluster, star, and other, constellation, magnitude range with unknown magnitudes listed last or left out, and apparent size), ordered by magnitude then id with a keyset cursor and the total count. `POST /discover` evaluates one batch: the bodies with the first one when asked, then up to 100 catalog objects of the filter; a fixed target whose meridian altitude stays more than 1° under the minimum altitude is rejected without a search, and the others are analyzed fully. It answers the observable candidates of the batch, best score first with ties broken by duration and identity, the number evaluated, the total, and the cursor of the next batch; it is never a global ranking until every batch has been read. It yields to the event loop every 10 candidates and stops when the request is aborted. Measured on the generated catalog (131,832 objects): the full listing in about 180 ms, a text search in about 35 ms, a new night in about 600 ms, then about 10 ms per analysis and 400 ms per batch of 100 candidates.
- Caches: the planning nights (8) and the analyses (256) are `LruCache` entries in memory, keyed by the site's place and zone, the evening, the target, the constraints, the requested duration, and the model version, and for a minor body also the identity of its trajectory's source (the provenance and the trajectory model version), so a new kernel set, policy, or orbit solution analyzes it again; nothing derived is written to the database.
- Plans (`ObservingPlan`, `GET /plans`, `GET`, `PUT`, `DELETE /plans/:id`, `POST /plans/:id/duplicate`): one catalog shared by every profile, tab, and browser. A plan holds a name, the evening date, a copy of the site (name, place, zone) with the id of the saved site it came from, which it never follows (deleting that site keeps the plan), the base constraints, and the items in order. An item holds its target and the name it had, a priority from 1 to 5 that never reorders or schedules anything, the requested continuous duration, an optional start chosen by the operator, optional constraints replacing the plan's field by field, and notes; the same target may appear twice. A save writes the whole document in one transaction from the revision it was edited from (0 creates it, ids come from the tab); another revision answers `409 conflict` with the current plan (`null` when it was deleted). A delete carries `?revision`. Each committed write publishes `observingPlan:updated` with the whole plan or `observingPlan:removed`, through `broadcast`.
- Preview (`POST /preview`, `ObservingPlanPreview`): analyzes every item of a plan document, saved or not, with its effective constraints (minor bodies one after the other, a failure of one never stopping the others), and diagnoses the schedule as one sequential lane (`diagnoseSchedule`): a block is its start plus its requested duration, and it is valid only inside one window, never across two. The problems are `unresolvedTarget`, `ephemerisUnavailable` (a minor body without a trajectory: its block is kept and never judged against windows nobody knows), `notObservable`, `insufficientContiguousTime`, `outsideNight`, `outsideValidWindow`, and `overlapsItem`; the totals are the requested and scheduled time, the unscheduled items, the observable items, the items with problems, and the scope (the stretches with the Sun below the plan's limit) with the part of it no block covers. Nothing moves, shortens, drops, or reorders an item, and conflicts may be saved.
